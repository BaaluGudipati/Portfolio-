Yelp is great if you already know your city

I'm an international student who lives between Raleigh and Hyderabad. This is my teardown of Yelp for people like me, plus five things I'd change and how I'd decide which to do first.

By Ishwarya Gandamsetty

When I landed in Raleigh for my M.S. in Computer Science in Fall 2025, Yelp was one of the first apps I downloaded. I didn't know anywhere. I didn't know which restaurants were good, which areas were fine, or who to ask. I figured Yelp would fill that gap.

Mostly it did. Then I went home to Hyderabad and it didn't. That gap is what this teardown is about. I've used the app in both cities, and I checked what I saw against Yelp's app store listings, its public company disclosures and its product docs. For every problem I found, I've tried to suggest a fix and a way to measure whether it worked. A list of complaints isn't much use to a product team on its own.

1.Product overview

Yelp is a marketplace with two sides. People use it to find and review local businesses: restaurants, plumbers, dentists, car shops. Businesses use it to get found, take bookings and leads, and keep an eye on their reputation.

The app matters less than what's behind it, which is more than 200 million reviews collected since 2004. Those reviews are why a Yelp result feels more trustworthy than a pin on a map.

Over the years Yelp has folded a bunch of separate jobs into one app: search and filters, reviews and photos, table reservations, delivery and pickup, and requesting quotes from service pros. It makes its money mostly from advertising, plus paid leads for home-service businesses. So consumers use it for free, and the paying customer is the local business buying visibility.

Where it's strong is the US, Canada, the UK and a few European countries. Yelp has tried to grow elsewhere (and pulled back from a number of international markets around 2016) but never got enough reviews in those places for the app to be useful. I think that one fact explains more of what follows than anything else.

2.Target users and goals

Yelp says its audience is anyone looking for a local business. In practice I see three kinds of people.

The local regular
Has lived in the city for years. Already knows which neighborhoods are which and what a fair price looks like. Opens Yelp to settle one decision, like which ramen place is open right now. Yelp is built for this person, and there are enough reviews and enough local context that it works.
The business owner
Runs a listing, replies to reviews, and pays for ads or leads. This is where Yelp's money comes from.
The newcomer
Just moved, or is traveling, or is an international student like me. A newcomer isn't only looking for the best place nearby. They're missing what a local just knows: who to trust, what "good" means here, and whether a place that looks fine online actually is.

What I wanted was pretty simple. I wanted to find food and services I could trust without having anyone to ask, and I didn't want to learn a different app every time I flew between the US and India.

Yelp is clearly designed around the first two people. It treats the newcomer as a slightly less experienced local regular, and most of my problems come from that.

3.User journey walkthrough

  1. Week one, RaleighTrusted, fast
  2. Trip to HyderabadSparse coverage
  3. Back in RaleighWorks well again

Week one in Raleigh

I open Yelp, allow location, and search "Indian restaurants near me." I get a list with star ratings, a price level and a few review snippets. I filter by rating and "open now," pick one, and skim the reviews.

That's where I get stuck. The reviews are in English and written by people I know nothing about. Is a 4-star "great food" from someone who's tried a dozen Indian places around the Triangle, or someone's first ever? I can't tell. I go anyway. If it's good I might check in or leave a review later. Haircut, oil change, dentist, it's the same loop: search, filter, skim, decide.

A visit home to Hyderabad

Then I visit home, open the same app out of habit, and search for a restaurant near my parents' place. The results are thin. A few listings, most with hardly any reviews, some with old hours or photos. That's not a glitch. It's just how far Yelp's coverage goes.

So I close it and open Zomato or Google Maps, like nearly everyone there does. My saved places, my review history, my sense of how to read a Yelp rating: none of it comes with me.

Back in Raleigh

When I'm back, Yelp works again. Nothing about the app changed. I did, twice in a few weeks, and Yelp doesn't notice. There's one version of the product, built for one kind of person in one kind of place, and everyone else works around it or stops using it.

4.What works well

In its home markets, Yelp deserves its spot on my home screen.

Filters
Distance, price, rating, hours and category filters are quick and they stack well. That matters when you just need to decide in the next two minutes.
Reviews, where there are lots of them
In a big US city there are enough reviews to spot the outliers and read between the lines. It's the closest thing to a local friend's opinion, which is the whole pitch.
Booking in the same app
You can reserve a table or order pickup from the page where you found the place. That saves a trip to another app.
Everything on one page
Hours, menu, photos, contact info and reviews are in one place, so I need fewer tabs open before I decide.
Saved lists
Saving places for later helps a lot when you're new. You build a list faster than you can visit anything.

None of this is a big surprise next to Yelp's competitors. But it's done well, and it's why I still open Yelp first in Raleigh.

5.Pain points and friction

Coverage drops off outside a few markets
This is the big one, and it isn't something a UI tweak fixes. Yelp only has real review depth in the US, Canada, the UK and parts of Europe. In India, Zomato and Google got there first and Yelp never caught up. If your life is split across two of those places, Yelp isn't a weaker version of itself in India. It feels like a different, much thinner app with the same icon.
Nothing follows you across borders
My saved lists, my review history, and my feel for what a 4-star means in this city all stay put. Yelp only cares where I am right now. It doesn't know where I'm from or where I'll be next month.
The dietary filters are too blunt
The vegetarian filter doesn't separate vegetarian from vegan, and there's nothing for Jain food. "Indian" is treated as one cuisine when it's a dozen or more regional ones. If you eat according to specific cultural or religious rules, the filter looks precise but isn't.
Reviews assume you know who's writing
A review is only useful if you can judge the reviewer. Locals do that without thinking. A newcomer can't, because Yelp doesn't show who's writing: a longtime resident, a tourist, someone who eats like me or someone who doesn't.
No help for people who just arrived
Someone who's lived here ten years and someone who arrived ten hours ago get the same first screen. There's no "I'm new here" mode with the basics: which areas are busy, some starter lists, how ratings tend to run in this city.
Ads make it harder to trust
This one isn't only about newcomers. Sponsored results take up more and more of the space organic ones used to have, and some service-business owners have complained publicly about the quality of Request a Quote leads. A newcomer is the worst placed to spot the difference, since they have no idea what a normal Yelp results page looks like.

6.Improvement proposals

Each idea is small enough for one product team to take on. None needs Yelp to reinvent itself, and each comes with a metric so you'd know if it worked.

1. Granular dietary and cultural filters

Go beyond vegetarian and vegan. Add Jain and halal, and regional cuisine tags (South Indian, North Indian, Indo-Chinese, for example). Let restaurants tag themselves, and check those tags against what reviewers say.

Success metric. How many food searchers use the new filters, and whether those searches lead to a visit more often than searches with the old filter.

2. "New to the city" mode

An opt-in mode. It could switch on when someone searches a lot in a market they've never used Yelp in, or when they tap "I'm new here." It would show starter lists for the area, a short neighborhood guide, and a quick note on how ratings tend to run locally.

Success metric. Seven-day retention and how many people book something in their first days, compared with similar new-market users who don't get the mode.

3. Reviewer context labels

An optional label on reviews that shows who wrote them, like "regular reviewer in this city" or "visiting." It gives a newcomer the context a local reads instinctively.

Success metric. How often people mark labeled reviews helpful compared with unlabeled ones, and fewer support disputes about reviews.

4. Cross-border profile continuity ("travel mode")

When Yelp sees someone has changed countries, it should bring their saved lists, review history and rating context with them. And if they've landed somewhere with thin coverage, it should say so instead of quietly showing worse results.

Success metric. Seven-day return rate after a country change, compared with today, when the drop-off is silent.

5. Local data partnerships in high-diaspora markets

Yelp shouldn't try another expensive launch in India. A better bet is a data-sharing or co-listing deal with a local platform that's already dominant. Start in cities with lots of returning students, visa holders and NRIs, who already use Yelp somewhere else.

Success metric. Growth in listings and reviews per listing in the pilot cities, and how often diaspora users go from search to action.

    Why this order

    Better dietary and cultural filters come first. It builds on a feature people already use, needs little beyond a tag list and restaurants tagging themselves, and you can measure it straight away. Onboarding mode and reviewer labels are next. Both go straight at the newcomer problem for a fair amount of work, and neither has to wait for Yelp to solve international coverage. Cross-border continuity is valuable but reaches deeper into accounts and data, so I'd do it after newcomer mode has been proven in one market. The partnership idea matters most to someone like me, because it's the only one that fixes the India side. It's also a multi-quarter bet across several teams, with real risk, so I'd treat it as a strategic project on the roadmap, not something for the next sprint.

    Closing note

    Yelp is really good for people who already know their city. It's a lot worse for people who don't yet, or who move between two. That's not a rare case. International students, people who've just moved, and families spread across countries are a big group, and they tend to need answers right now. Fixing this wouldn't turn Yelp into a different company. It would mean giving the newcomer a place in a product that currently only has room for one kind of user.
